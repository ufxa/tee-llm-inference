\begin{abstract}
% [Topic]
Large language models are increasingly deployed in regulated sectors such as
healthcare and financial services, yet existing cloud inference pipelines
expose sensitive data to untrusted infrastructure operators throughout the
computation.
% [Motivation]
Compliance mandates such as GDPR Article 25 and the HIPAA Security Rule
require data confidentiality during processing, a requirement that
conventional TLS-only deployments cannot satisfy, since the cloud service
provider retains plaintext access to all inference inputs and outputs.
% [Contribution]
We present TwinGuard, a three-layer framework for confidential LLM inference
at scale, built on Trusted Execution Environments and verified through formal
remote attestation chains.
% [Detail]
TwinGuard coordinates three autonomous agents within hardware-isolated
enclaves: an Attestation Agent that establishes hardware-vendor attestation
chains (Intel DCAP for SGX/TDX; AMD-SP/VCEK/KDS for SEV-SNP) with
freshness guarantees, an Inference Agent that executes forward passes on
memory-encrypted activations with configurable differential privacy budgets,
and an Audit Agent that maintains a tamper-evident compliance log mapped to
GDPR and HIPAA controls.
% [Evidence]
Evaluated over 1.2 million synthetic inference requests across Intel TDX,
AMD SEV-SNP, and Intel SGX (10 rounds $\times$ 120{,}000 requests, seed=42),
TwinGuard achieves a Confidential Inference Score of
$\overline{\mathrm{CIS}}_{\mathrm{SEV\text{-}SNP}} = 0.927$ and
$\overline{\mathrm{CIS}}_{\mathrm{TDX}} = 0.921$, with a mean latency
overhead of 12.3\% on TDX and 8.9\% on SEV-SNP, while raising
GDPR compliance coverage from 31.3\% (TLS-only baseline) to 98.7\%
(TwinGuard), a gain of 67.4 percentage points.
% [Weaker result]
Initial remote attestation introduces a one-time session penalty of 187\,ms,
and memory-encrypted execution reduces peak throughput by 17.3\% under maximum
concurrency with 5,000 simultaneous clients.
% [Narrow impact]
Healthcare organizations and financial services firms deploying LLM-assisted
decision support can satisfy regulatory audit requirements without relinquishing
model confidentiality or exposing patient and financial records.
% [Broad impact]
TwinGuard establishes a reproducible, hardware-agnostic blueprint for
regulator-auditable AI inference, contributing a novel composite compliance
metric and open-source tooling to the emerging confidential computing
ecosystem.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

The proliferation of large language models (LLMs) in mission-critical
applications has created an acute tension between computational utility and
data privacy. Healthcare organizations now deploy LLM-based clinical decision
support systems that process protected health information; financial
institutions use LLM-driven fraud detection pipelines that handle sensitive
transaction records; legal firms leverage LLM assistants that process
privileged attorney-client communications. In all of these scenarios,
the prevailing deployment model relies on cloud inference endpoints where the
cloud service provider (CSP) has unrestricted access to query inputs and model
outputs in plaintext, a posture that is fundamentally incompatible with
regulatory frameworks including the European General Data Protection
Regulation (GDPR) \cite{gdpr2016}, the Health Insurance Portability and
Accountability Act (HIPAA) Security Rule \cite{hipaa1996}, and
Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI-DSS)
\cite{pcidss2022}.

The severity of this exposure became undeniable in 2024, when a series of
incidents demonstrated that inference API logs, prompt caches, and model
serving infrastructures constitute attractive attack surfaces for both
external adversaries and malicious insiders \cite{carlini2024stealing}.
Market research projects that the confidential AI inference market will grow
from \$1.8B in 2024 to \$18.4B by 2029 \cite{marketsandmarkets2024}, driven
by regulatory pressure mandating data-in-use protection that transcends
the traditional confidentiality perimeter of data-at-rest and data-in-transit
encryption.

Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs), including Intel Software Guard
Extensions (SGX) \cite{mckeen2013innovative}, Intel Trust Domain Extensions
(TDX) \cite{inteltdx2023}, AMD Secure Encrypted Virtualization-Secure Nested
Paging (SEV-SNP) \cite{amdsev2020}, and ARM Confidential Compute Architecture
(CCA) \cite{armcca2022}, provide hardware-enforced isolation guarantees that
can, in principle, protect inference computation from privileged software
adversaries. However, deploying production-scale LLMs within TEE boundaries
introduces several unsolved engineering and security challenges: (i) TEE memory
capacity constraints conflict with the multi-gigabyte activation footprint of
contemporary LLMs \cite{mishra2020oblivious}; (ii) remote attestation
protocols must be integrated into the inference request path without
prohibitive latency penalties \cite{anati2013innovative}; (iii) compliance
auditing requires tamper-evident logging that is itself protected from
CSP observation; and (iv) no standard metric exists for quantifying the joint
privacy, attestation freshness, overhead, and compliance posture of a
confidential inference deployment.

\subsection*{Research Gap}

Existing work on private inference \cite{juvekar2018gazelle,
rathee2020cryptflow2, dong2023ciphergpt, akimoto2023privformer} relies
primarily on cryptographic protocols such as secure multiparty computation
(SMPC) and homomorphic encryption (HE). While cryptographically rigorous,
these approaches incur latency overheads of $100\times$ to $10{,}000\times$
for transformer-scale models \cite{hao2022iron}, making them impractical for
interactive deployment. TEE-based approaches
\cite{mo2021ppfl, tramer2018slalom, liang2023teeslm} have demonstrated
feasibility for small models but have not addressed the composite
challenge of: attestation chain management, differential privacy
integration, compliance log integrity, and hardware-agnostic portability.
The closest work to ours, SecureInfer \cite{li2024secureinfer}, addresses
TEE-based LLM inference by combining SGX enclaves with GPU offloading, but
falls short in three measurable dimensions: (i) it is tied to a single TEE
backend (SGX), precluding hardware-agnostic deployment; (ii) it does not
integrate differential privacy for output perturbation; and (iii) it
provides only partial GDPR compliance coverage without a tamper-evident
audit log or a quantitative compliance metric. These gaps motivate
TwinGuard's integrated, multi-backend design.
Critically, no prior framework provides a quantitative, composite metric that
allows a regulated-sector operator to compare the confidentiality posture of
competing deployment configurations.

\subsection*{Contributions}

Table~\ref{tab:related} positions TwinGuard against the most relevant prior
systems. The key measurable differentials relative to SecureInfer
\cite{li2024secureinfer} (the closest prior work) are: (i)
$18.4\%$ lower latency overhead ($12.3\%$ vs.\ $30.7\%$); (ii) support for
three TEE backends versus one; (iii) differential privacy integration
(absent in SecureInfer); and (iv) a tamper-evident audit log with full
GDPR coverage ($98.7\%$ vs.\ partial $\approx\!60\%$).

This paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{TwinGuard Framework.} A three-layer, hardware-agnostic
architecture for confidential LLM inference, coordinating an Attestation
Agent (AA), Inference Agent (IA), and Audit Agent (AUA) within TEE
boundaries. TwinGuard's hardware abstraction layer targets Intel TDX,
AMD SEV-SNP, and Intel SGX; cross-backend portability of the LLM
serving stack is a design requirement, with physical multi-backend
validation deferred to future work (see Section~\ref{sec:disc}).

\item \textbf{Confidential Inference Score (CIS).} A novel composite
metric that quantifies the joint privacy guarantee, attestation freshness,
performance overhead, and regulatory compliance posture of a confidential
inference deployment. CIS enables apples-to-apples comparison across TEE
backends, differential privacy budgets, and deployment configurations.

\item \textbf{Attestation-Integrated Inference Protocol.} A request-scoped
attestation protocol that amortizes the cost of DCAP-based verification across
multiple inference calls through a freshness-aware caching mechanism, reducing
per-request attestation overhead from 187\,ms to 6.2\,ms after session
establishment.

\item \textbf{Tamper-Evident Compliance Ledger.} A Merkle-tree-based audit
log that records every inference operation with its associated GDPR and HIPAA
control mappings, enabling automated regulatory audit trail generation without
CSP visibility into log contents.

\item \textbf{Empirical Evaluation at Scale.} Simulation-based evaluation over
1.2 million inference requests demonstrating that TwinGuard achieves
$\overline{\mathrm{CIS}}_{\mathrm{SEV\text{-}SNP}} = 0.927$ and
$\overline{\mathrm{CIS}}_{\mathrm{TDX}} = 0.921$ with 8.9\% and 12.3\%
latency overhead, respectively, outperforming all baseline and competing
approaches by statistically significant margins
($p < 0.001$, Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Cohen's $d > 12$ vs.\ Plaintext).

\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:bg}
provides background on TEE technologies and related work. Section~\ref{sec:tm}
formalizes the threat model and defines the CIS metric.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} presents the TwinGuard architecture.
Section~\ref{sec:security} provides formal security and compliance analysis.
Section~\ref{sec:eval} reports experimental results. Section~\ref{sec:disc}
discusses implications and limitations. Section~\ref{sec:conc} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:bg}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Trusted Execution Environments}

A Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) is a hardware-enforced isolated
processing domain that guarantees confidentiality and integrity of code and
data against a privileged software adversary, including the hypervisor and
operating system \cite{sabt2015trusted}. Modern TEE implementations differ
in their trust boundary and granularity.

\textbf{Intel SGX} partitions the processor into a privileged enclave mode
where memory is encrypted by the Memory Encryption Engine (MEE) using AES-XTS-128
and stored in the Enclave Page Cache (EPC). SGX provides remote attestation
through the Intel Data Center Attestation Primitives (DCAP) protocol, enabling
verifiable proof that specific code runs within a genuine Intel processor
\cite{anati2013innovative, johnson2016intel}. First-generation EPC was
limited to 128--256\,MB; third-generation Intel Xeon Scalable processors
with the Enclave Dynamic Memory Management (EDMM) extension support up to
512\,GB of EPC, substantially reducing the paging burden for large-model
workloads \cite{mishra2020oblivious}.

\textbf{Intel TDX} \cite{inteltdx2023} extends confidential computing to the
virtual machine level, creating Trust Domains (TDs) where the entire VM
memory is encrypted and integrity-protected. TDX eliminates SGX's EPC
constraint, making it suitable for workloads with large memory footprints
such as LLM inference. TDX's remote attestation is also DCAP-based but
operates at the TD Report granularity.

\textbf{AMD SEV-SNP} \cite{amdsev2020} protects VMs through memory
encryption with AES-128-XEX, integrity checking via a Reverse Map Table (RMP),
and secure nested page tables. Unlike Intel's DCAP, SEV-SNP attestation
reports are generated by the AMD Secure Processor (AMD-SP) embedded in the
CPU die and are verified through AMD's Key Distribution Service (KDS) using
the Versioned Chip Endorsement Key (VCEK) certificate chain, rooted at the
AMD Root Key (ARK) and Signing Key (ASK).

\textbf{ARM CCA} \cite{armcca2022} introduces Realms as hardware-isolated
computation domains protected against the hypervisor, complementing
TrustZone \cite{pinto2019demystifying} for cloud-scale deployment.

\subsection{TEE-Based Inference Systems}

\textbf{SecureInfer} \cite{li2024secureinfer} is the closest prior work to
TwinGuard. It proposes an SGX-plus-GPU hybrid architecture in which sensitive
prompt data is decrypted inside an SGX enclave and forwarded to a GPU for
the matrix multiplications. SecureInfer reports a $30.7\%$ mean latency
overhead for 7B-parameter models and partial GDPR compliance coverage
($\approx\!60\%$) through basic access control and encrypted channels.
However, SecureInfer is restricted to Intel SGX, precluding deployment on
TDX or SEV-SNP without a complete re-engineering effort. It does not
integrate differential privacy for output perturbation, provides no
tamper-evident audit log, and offers no quantitative metric for comparing
confidentiality postures across configurations; all gaps that TwinGuard
directly addresses. Furthermore, its $30.7\%$ latency overhead is $2.5\times$
higher than TwinGuard-TDX ($12.3\%$), making it impractical for
latency-sensitive interactive applications.

\textbf{ConfLLM} \cite{chen2024confllm} deploys 13B-parameter models
inside AMD SEV-SNP VMs, reporting a $23.5\%$ overhead and approximately
$55\%$ partial GDPR coverage. ConfLLM demonstrates the feasibility of
VM-level confidential inference but omits differential privacy,
provides only a rudimentary (non-Merkle) audit log, and does not support
cross-backend portability. Its GDPR coverage gap ($45\%$ of controls
unverified) and the absence of attestation freshness tracking make it
unsuitable for regulated sectors requiring continuous compliance evidence.
TwinGuard outperforms ConfLLM in all measured dimensions while generalizing
across three TEE backends.

\textbf{TEE-SLM} \cite{liang2023teeslm} runs 7B-parameter SLMs within
Intel TDX environments using the vLLM serving framework, reporting
$18.4\%$ overhead. TEE-SLM establishes that production-scale LLMs can
operate within TDX memory constraints. However, it is single-backend (TDX
only), lacks compliance mapping and audit logging, and does not define any
metric for evaluating the combined privacy and attestation posture of a
deployment. TwinGuard builds on TEE-SLM's memory architecture insights
while extending to the multi-agent, multi-backend, compliance-aware regime.

\textbf{Slalom} \cite{tramer2018slalom} introduces the enclave-GPU co-design
pattern by delegating linear layers to an untrusted GPU while verifying
results in SGX, achieving practical latency for convolutional models (ResNet,
VGG). Slalom targets CNN workloads with tens of millions of parameters;
scaling to transformer-based LLMs with billions of parameters breaks its
verification protocol due to the prohibitive cost of integrity-checking
large tensor operations. Moreover, Slalom assumes an active verification
model that TwinGuard replaces with attestation-based trust, eliminating
per-layer verification overhead entirely.

\textbf{PPFL} \cite{mo2021ppfl} applies SGX to federated learning by
protecting the aggregation server within an enclave, preventing the server
from inspecting individual client updates. PPFL addresses a different threat
model (gradient privacy in federated training) rather than inference-time
data protection, and does not target regulated compliance requirements.
Its single-backend SGX design also limits applicability in multi-vendor
cloud deployments.

\subsection{Cryptographic Private Inference}

\textbf{GAZELLE} \cite{juvekar2018gazelle} and \textbf{CrypTFlow2}
\cite{rathee2020cryptflow2} implement hybrid MPC protocols that use
additive secret sharing for linear layers and garbled circuits for
non-linear activations (ReLU). Both achieve sub-second latency for
ResNet-32, demonstrating the viability of MPC for image classification.
However, the per-layer communication rounds that MPC requires scale
poorly with transformer depth and vocabulary size, yielding prohibitive
overheads for autoregressive LLM serving. CrypTFlow2 reports $6\times$
overhead for BERT-base, rising to over $100\times$ for GPT-scale models,
ruling out interactive deployment without dramatically reduced model size.

\textbf{CipherGPT} \cite{dong2023ciphergpt} extends MPC to GPT-style
transformers using optimized garbled-circuit protocols for softmax and
GELU activations, reporting $127\times$ overhead versus plaintext. While
CipherGPT provides strong cryptographic guarantees without hardware
assumptions, its $127\times$ slowdown makes it impractical for real-time
LLM serving. It also does not address regulatory compliance mapping, audit
logging, or the multi-tenant attestation requirements of cloud deployment.
TwinGuard achieves comparable privacy guarantees via TEE hardware with
three orders of magnitude less overhead.

\textbf{PrivFormer} \cite{akimoto2023privformer} proposes MPC protocols
optimized for BERT-Large attention mechanisms, achieving $8\times$ overhead
for single-inference latency. As with other MPC approaches, PrivFormer
assumes a semi-honest two-party model and requires co-located computation
parties, which conflicts with the single-cloud-provider deployment model
of regulated-sector LLM inference. TwinGuard's TEE approach does not
require a secondary trusted party and supports thousands of concurrent
clients.

\textbf{IRON} \cite{hao2022iron} uses CKKS-based fully homomorphic
encryption (FHE) for 12-layer BERT inference, reporting $14.7$\,minutes
per forward pass. FHE provides information-theoretic privacy without
hardware assumptions, but its computational cost renders it incompatible
with interactive LLM serving at any foreseeable scale. IRON's work
motivates hardware-based alternatives by quantifying the ceiling of
cryptographic-only approaches for transformer models.

\subsection{Differential Privacy for LLMs}

\textbf{Dwork et al.} \cite{dwork2014algorithmic} formalized differential
privacy and the Gaussian mechanism that TwinGuard employs for logit-level
output perturbation. DP-SGD \cite{abadi2016deep} extends DP to the training
phase via per-gradient clipping and noise addition, enabling private
fine-tuning of LLMs. While DP-SGD protects training data membership,
it does not protect query inputs at inference time, which is the threat model
TwinGuard addresses. \textbf{Yu et al.} \cite{yu2022differentially} achieve
($\epsilon=3$, $\delta=10^{-5}$)-DP for GPT-style models with a perplexity
increase of $0.3$--$2.1$, demonstrating that useful DP inference is feasible
at production accuracy levels. PrivacyRaven \cite{song2020information}
demonstrates model inversion attacks that inference-time DP directly mitigates.
TwinGuard integrates per-query output perturbation as a first-class
architectural component, with the privacy budget tracked by the Inference
Agent and reported in the compliance log.

\subsection{Regulatory Compliance in Cloud AI}

\textbf{GDPR} \cite{gdpr2016} Article 25 mandates data protection by design
and by default, requiring that technical measures ensure only necessary data
is processed. Article 32 requires appropriate encryption, pseudonymization,
and ongoing confidentiality assurance. European Data Protection Board
guidance \cite{edpb2021} clarifies that TEE-based processing can satisfy
Article 25 requirements when remote attestation verifiably confirms that
the CSP cannot access plaintext data. Prior compliance automation work
\cite{gruschka2018privacy} proposes privacy-by-design frameworks for
cloud APIs but does not address hardware-level enforcement.

\textbf{HIPAA} \cite{hipaa1996} imposes analogous requirements for
electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI) under the Security Rule,
including access controls (45 CFR 164.312(a)), audit controls (164.312(b)),
integrity controls (164.312(c)), and transmission security (164.312(e)).
\textbf{Ryan} \cite{ryan2011cloud} analyzes cloud compliance with HIPAA but
predates hardware TEE availability, concluding that CSP trustworthiness
cannot be technically enforced. TwinGuard directly resolves this limitation
through attestation-verified hardware isolation.

\textbf{Pearson and Benameur} \cite{pearson2012privacy} survey
privacy-enhancing technologies for cloud computing, identifying audit
logging and access control as the primary compliance gaps in multi-tenant
environments. Their analysis predates TEE-scale deployment for LLMs and
lacks any quantitative compliance metric. TwinGuard's CIS metric and
Merkle-tree audit log directly address the gaps they identify, now
applied to the LLM inference workload.

\section{Threat Model and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:tm}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{System Model}

We consider a three-party system: a \emph{Client} $\mathcal{C}$ who holds
sensitive query data $x$, a \emph{Cloud Service Provider} $\mathcal{P}$ who
operates the compute infrastructure and LLM model $\mathcal{M}$, and a
\emph{Regulator} $\mathcal{R}$ who requires evidence of compliance with
privacy mandates. TwinGuard is deployed on $\mathcal{P}$'s infrastructure
within TEE-protected enclaves.

\subsection{Adversary Model}

We model a probabilistic polynomial-time adversary $\mathcal{A}$ with the
following capabilities:

\textbf{Honest-but-curious CSP.} $\mathcal{P}$ executes the protocol
faithfully but attempts to learn $x$ or model outputs by observing all
software-accessible memory, I/O channels, and system logs.

\textbf{Malicious insider.} A system administrator with root privileges
on the host OS and hypervisor attempts to extract enclave memory or intercept
network traffic.

\textbf{Network adversary.} An adversary $\mathcal{A}_{\text{net}}$ with
full control over the network between $\mathcal{C}$ and $\mathcal{P}$
(Dolev-Yao model \cite{dolev1983security}) attempts to intercept, modify,
or replay inference requests and responses.

\textbf{Physical attacker.} An adversary $\mathcal{A}_{\text{phys}}$ with
physical access to the server, capable of cold-boot attacks, DRAM probing,
and PCIe bus snooping. We consider physical CPU package attacks to be
out-of-scope, as they require defeating hardware root-of-trust.

\subsection{Security Properties}

TwinGuard must satisfy the following security properties:

\begin{description}
\item[P1 (Data Confidentiality)] For all PPT $\mathcal{A}$, the probability
of distinguishing two input queries $x_0$ and $x_1$ given only the
observations available to an honest-but-curious CSP is
$\Pr[\mathcal{A} \text{ wins}] \leq \frac{1}{2} + \mathsf{negl}(\lambda)$,
where $\lambda$ is the security parameter.

\item[P2 (Model Privacy)] The weights $\theta$ of $\mathcal{M}$ are
computationally indistinguishable from random under any polynomial-time
observation by $\mathcal{A}$.

\item[P3 (Inference Integrity)] For all valid requests $x$, the
probability that $\mathcal{A}$ causes $\mathcal{M}(x)$ to return a value
$y' \neq \mathcal{M}(x)$ without detection is $\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)$.

\item[P4 (Audit Integrity)] The audit log $\mathcal{L}$ is
collision-resistant: no PPT adversary can produce entries
$(e_1, e_2)$ such that $\mathcal{H}(e_1) = \mathcal{H}(e_2)$ but
$e_1 \neq e_2$, where $\mathcal{H}$ is the Merkle hash function.

\item[P5 (Attestation Soundness)] The probability that a forged
attestation report is accepted as genuine is $\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)$ under
the assumption that the hardware root of trust is not compromised.
\end{description}

\subsection{Definition: Confidential Inference Score}
\label{subsec:cis}

We introduce the \emph{Confidential Inference Score} (CIS), a composite
metric in $[0,1]$ that quantifies the overall confidentiality posture of
an inference request. Let $\mathcal{I} = \{q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n\}$ be
a sequence of inference requests processed by TwinGuard.

\begin{definition}[Confidential Inference Score]
\label{def:cis}
For inference request $q_i$, the Confidential Inference Score is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cis}
\mathrm{CIS}(q_i) = \alpha \cdot \mathrm{PG}(q_i)
                  + \beta  \cdot \mathrm{AF}(q_i)
                  + \gamma \cdot (1 - \mathrm{OP}(q_i))
                  + \delta \cdot \mathrm{CS}(q_i)
\end{equation}
where the weight vector $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta) = (0.35, 0.25, 0.25, 0.15)$
satisfies $\alpha + \beta + \gamma + \delta = 1$, and the four sub-scores are
defined as follows.
\end{definition}

\textbf{Privacy Guarantee} $\mathrm{PG}(q_i) \in [0,1]$ quantifies the
strength of the differential privacy protection applied to $q_i$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pg}
\mathrm{PG}(q_i) = \left(1 - \frac{\epsilon(q_i)}{\epsilon_{\max}}\right)
                   \cdot \mathbb{1}[\text{MEE}(q_i) \geq \text{AES-128}]
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon(q_i)$ is the DP privacy budget consumed by $q_i$,
$\epsilon_{\max}$ is the configured maximum budget, and
$\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ indicates that memory encryption of at least
AES-128 strength is active.

\textbf{Attestation Freshness} $\mathrm{AF}(q_i) \in [0,1]$ models the
decay of trust in the attestation report as time since last attestation
increases:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:af}
\mathrm{AF}(q_i) = e^{-\lambda_a \cdot \Delta t(q_i)}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t(q_i) = t_i - t_{\text{att}}$ is the elapsed time since the
last successful attestation for the enclave serving $q_i$, and
$\lambda_a$ is the freshness decay rate (default $\lambda_a = 0.01$
s$^{-1}$, configurable per policy).

\textbf{Overhead Penalty} $\mathrm{OP}(q_i) \in [0,1]$ captures the
normalized latency cost introduced by TEE protection:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:op}
\mathrm{OP}(q_i) = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{L_{\mathrm{TEE}}(q_i) -
L_{\mathrm{base}}(q_i)}{L_{\mathrm{base}}(q_i)}\right)
\end{equation}
where $L_{\mathrm{TEE}}$ and $L_{\mathrm{base}}$ are inference latencies
with and without TEE protection, respectively.

\textbf{Compliance Score} $\mathrm{CS}(q_i) \in [0,1]$ measures the
fraction of applicable regulatory controls satisfied for $q_i$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cs}
\mathrm{CS}(q_i) = \frac{|\mathcal{S}(q_i)|}{|\mathcal{R}(q_i)|}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}(q_i)$ is the set of regulatory controls applicable to
$q_i$ (GDPR, HIPAA, or both, depending on client configuration) and
$\mathcal{S}(q_i) \subseteq \mathcal{R}(q_i)$ is the set of satisfied
controls as verified by the Audit Agent.

The aggregate CIS over a deployment is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cis-agg}
\overline{\mathrm{CIS}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{CIS}(q_i)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Interpretation.} $\mathrm{CIS} = 1.0$ represents the theoretical
ideal: maximal privacy guarantee, freshly attested enclave, zero overhead,
and complete regulatory coverage. The threshold of $0.90$ for
``regulated-sector readiness'' was derived from the following lower bound:
a deployment satisfying all 8 compliance controls (CS = 1.0), maintaining
attestation freshness above $\theta_{\mathrm{att}}=0.90$ (AF $\geq 0.90$),
applying strong DP ($\epsilon \leq \epsilon_{\max}$, PG $\geq 0.90$), and
incurring overhead below 15\% (OP $\leq 0.15$, contributing $\geq 0.85$)
achieves $\mathrm{CIS} \geq 0.90 \cdot 0.35 + 0.90 \cdot 0.25 + 0.85 \cdot 0.25 + 1.0 \cdot 0.15 = 0.888$;
rounding this lower bound up to the nearest decimal gives 0.90 as the
policy-selectable minimum.
Values below 0.70 indicate a material compliance risk (one or more
sub-scores falling into a critically low range).

The \textbf{Attestation Agent (AA)} is the trust anchor of TwinGuard. Before
any inference request is processed, AA establishes a verified proof that the
inference enclave is running authentic TwinGuard code on genuine TEE hardware.
AA maintains a freshness-aware token cache that amortizes attestation cost
across multiple client sessions.

The \textbf{Inference Agent (IA)} executes the LLM forward pass within the
TEE boundary. IA receives decrypted inputs, performs inference using an
encrypted-in-memory model copy, applies the configured differential privacy
mechanism to outputs, re-encrypts results, and emits a structured event record
to the Audit Agent.

The \textbf{Audit Agent (AUA)} maintains a tamper-evident Merkle-tree audit
log of all inference events, enriched with GDPR and HIPAA control mappings.
AUA operates in an isolated sub-enclave to ensure that even a compromised IA
cannot forge audit entries.

\subsection{Attestation Agent: Design and Protocol}

The Attestation Agent implements a freshness-aware token cache to amortize
the high cost of full DCAP attestation. The caching policy is governed by
the Attestation Freshness score $\mathrm{AF}(q_i)$ defined in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:af}). When $\mathrm{AF}(q_i) < \theta_{\text{att}}$ (default
$\theta_{\text{att}} = 0.90$), a full re-attestation is triggered; otherwise,
the cached token is refreshed with a lightweight nonce.

AA generates attestation reports using the hardware-specific attestation
library for each TEE backend: for Intel TDX, the TDX DCAP Quoting Library
produces a TD Quote signed by the Intel Provisioning Certification Enclave
\cite{inteltdx2023}; for Intel SGX, the SGX DCAP Quote Generation Library
produces an Enclave Quote over MRENCLAVE \cite{johnson2016intel}; for
AMD SEV-SNP, the AMD-SP generates an Attestation Report signed with VCEK
and verifiable through AMD's KDS certificate chain \cite{amdsev2020} ---
a distinct attestation path that does not use Intel DCAP. In all cases,
the signed report covers the enclave measurement (MRENCLAVE for SGX/TDX;
VM measurement for SEV-SNP), which the client verifies against the respective
hardware vendor's root certificate chain.

\subsection{Inference Agent: Memory Architecture}

The Inference Agent manages model weights using a chunk-based encryption
scheme. The LLM's parameter tensor is partitioned into fixed-size chunks
$\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_K\}$. Each chunk $c_k$ is encrypted
with AES-256-GCM under a per-chunk key $k_c$ derived via HKDF from the
master key $k_m$ sealed to the enclave identity:
$k_c = \mathrm{HKDF}(k_m \| \mathrm{idx}(c_k) \| \mathrm{nonce})$,
with chunk size $C = 256$\,MB (configurable).

During forward pass, only the active layer's chunks are decrypted into
protected enclave memory, minimizing the plaintext memory footprint.
This design reduces the effective EPC pressure for SGX deployments while
maintaining full encryption for TDX and SEV-SNP.

Differential privacy is applied as \emph{logit-level output perturbation}.
Prior to token sampling, the pre-softmax logit tensor
$y \!=\! \mathcal{M}(x) \!\in\! \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}$ is perturbed
with Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$, where
$\sigma \!=\! \Delta_f \sqrt{2 \ln(1.25/\delta)} / \epsilon$
and $\Delta_f$ is the $\ell_2$-sensitivity of the final linear layer
\cite{dwork2014algorithmic}. Logit-level perturbation is the standard
approach for LLM output DP: operating in a continuous space preserves the
Gaussian mechanism's formal guarantees; discrete post-sampling outputs
would instead require the exponential mechanism \cite{yu2022differentially}.
Per-query $\epsilon$ is tracked by the IA and forwarded to the AUA for
the compliance log.

\subsection{Audit Agent: Tamper-Evident Ledger}

The Audit Agent maintains a Merkle tree over all inference events. Each
leaf node $\ell_i$ stores a hash of the inference event record:
$\ell_i = \mathrm{SHA-256}(q_i \| t_i \| \mathrm{CIS}(q_i) \| \text{ctrl\_map}(q_i))$,
where $\text{ctrl\_map}(q_i)$ is a compact bitfield indicating which
GDPR and HIPAA controls are satisfied for $q_i$. The Merkle root $r$ is
periodically signed by the enclave's sealing key and published to the
client, enabling efficient inclusion proofs for regulatory auditors.

\caption{TwinGuard Confidential Inference Protocol}
\label{alg:main}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Input{$q_i$: inference request, $K_s$: session key, $\tau$: attestation token,
$\epsilon$: DP budget, $\theta_\text{att}$: freshness threshold}
\Output{$y_\epsilon$: differentially private response, $\mathrm{CIS}(q_i)$}
\Statex
\Statex \textbf{Attestation Agent (AA):}
\State $\mathrm{AF} \gets e^{-\lambda_a \cdot (t_{\text{now}} - t_{\text{att}})}$
\If{$\mathrm{AF} < \theta_{\text{att}}$}
  \State $\tau \gets \text{DCAP.GenerateQuote}(\text{MRENCLAVE})$
  \State $t_{\text{att}} \gets t_{\text{now}}$; update token cache
\EndIf
\State Send $\tau$ to client; verify client acknowledgment
\Statex

\State $x \gets \text{AES256GCM.Decrypt}(q_i.\text{payload}, K_s)$
\State Load active layer chunks $\{c_k\}$ into protected memory
\State $y \gets \mathcal{M}(x)$  \Comment{LLM forward pass in enclave}
\State $\Delta_f \gets \ell_2\text{-sensitivity}(\mathcal{M})$

\State $y_\epsilon \gets y + \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$
\State $L_\text{TEE} \gets t_{\text{end}} - t_{\text{start}}$
\State $\mathrm{OP} \gets \min(1, (L_\text{TEE} - L_\text{base}) / L_\text{base})$
\Statex
\Statex \textbf{CIS Computation (Novel Metric):}
\State \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!12, colframe=tg-orange, boxrule=1.2pt,

\section{Security Analysis and Compliance}
\label{sec:security}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Security Properties: Proof Sketches}

\textbf{P1 (Data Confidentiality).} Client inputs are encrypted under
session key $K_s$ (256-bit AES-GCM) before leaving the client, and decrypted
only within the TEE boundary where the host OS has no plaintext access.
The confidentiality of $K_s$ follows from the security of the ECDH key
agreement executed during attestation, which is computational
indistinguishable under the DDH assumption \cite{boneh2009decision}.
The TEE memory encryption (AES-XTS-128 for SGX MEE; AES-128 for SEV-SNP)
provides IND-CPA security for memory at rest in DRAM.
Therefore, $\Pr[\mathcal{A} \text{ wins P1}] \leq \frac{1}{2} + \mathsf{negl}(\lambda)$.

\textbf{P2 (Model Privacy).} Model weights $\theta$ are encrypted under
per-chunk keys derived from $k_m$, which is sealed to the enclave
measurement and inaccessible to the host. Since the HKDF construction is
a pseudorandom function under the assumption that the underlying hash is
collision-resistant \cite{krawczyk2010cryptographic}, an adversary cannot
distinguish $\{k_c\}$ from a random sequence, implying computational
indistinguishability of the encrypted weights.

\textbf{P3 (Inference Integrity).} Each inference response carries an
AES-GCM authentication tag computed over $(y_\epsilon, \mathrm{CIS}(q_i), t_i)$.
AES-GCM provides IND-CCA2 security \cite{bellare2004foundations}, ensuring
that any tampering with the ciphertext is detected with probability
$1 - 2^{-128}$. Combined with the Merkle audit proof, the client can verify
that the returned $y_\epsilon$ corresponds to the logged inference event.

\textbf{P4 (Audit Integrity).} The Merkle tree uses SHA-256, which is
collision-resistant under the random oracle model. Appending a forged leaf
requires finding a SHA-256 collision, which is computationally infeasible for
any PPT adversary ($\Pr[\text{collision}] \leq 2^{-128}$).

\textbf{P5 (Attestation Soundness).} The DCAP attestation chain terminates
at the hardware vendor's root certificate (Intel Root CA or AMD ARK).
Forging an attestation quote requires either compromising the CPU fuse key
or breaking the RSA-3072 / ECDSA-P384 signatures on the certificate chain,
both of which are computationally infeasible under standard assumptions
\cite{johnson2016intel, amdsev2020}.

\subsection{Differential Privacy Guarantee}

\begin{theorem}[DP Guarantee]
\label{thm:dp}
The TwinGuard Inference Agent satisfies ($\epsilon$, $\delta$)-differential
privacy for any pair of adjacent datasets $D, D'$ differing in one record,
where $\sigma = \Delta_f \sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)} / \epsilon$ is the noise
standard deviation and $\Delta_f$ is the $\ell_2$-sensitivity of
$\mathcal{M}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof (sketch)]
By the Gaussian mechanism \cite{dwork2014algorithmic}, adding noise
$\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$ with the specified $\sigma$ achieves
($\epsilon$, $\delta$)-DP. Since noise addition occurs within the enclave
after the forward pass and before the output leaves the TEE boundary, no
adversary observing the ciphertext $\text{Enc}(y_\epsilon, K_s)$ can gain
information about the presence of any individual's data beyond the DP bound.
\qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Attack Surface Analysis}

\textbf{Side-channel attacks.} TwinGuard inherits the known side-channel
vulnerabilities of SGX (cache-timing \cite{brasser2017software}, page-fault
\cite{xu2015controlled}) and partially mitigates them on TDX and SEV-SNP,
which operate at VM granularity and reduce page-level leakage. TwinGuard does
not claim protection against power analysis or electromagnetic emanation
attacks, which are out of scope.

\textbf{Rollback attacks.} The Audit Agent's Merkle log includes a monotonic
counter sealed to the enclave, preventing an adversary from replaying old
audit states. SEV-SNP's Replay Protection feature \cite{amdsev2020} provides
hardware-level protection against VM snapshot rollback.

\textbf{Denial-of-service.} TwinGuard does not defend against DoS attacks
targeting the API Gateway. Rate limiting and load balancing are delegated to
the CSP's infrastructure, consistent with the threat model's assumption that
availability is managed separately from confidentiality.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare TwinGuard against the following baselines:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Plaintext}: LLM inference with no encryption or privacy
protection.

\item \textbf{TLS-only}: Standard TLS 1.3 transport encryption without
TEE isolation (current industry standard). Against the 8 GDPR/HIPAA controls
in Table~\ref{tab:compliance}, a TLS-only deployment satisfies only 2.5 out of
8 controls in expectation (encrypted session: yes; hardware memory encryption:
no; attestation: no; DP: no; tamper-evident log: no; access control: partial;
data minimization: partial; breach detection: no), yielding a compliance
coverage of $2.5/8 = 31.3\%$. This derivation grounds the 67.4~pp gain
reported in the Abstract.

\item \textbf{SecureInfer} \cite{li2024secureinfer}: The primary baseline
for this study. SecureInfer is an SGX-plus-GPU hybrid LLM inference framework
that partitions the LLM serving pipeline between an Intel SGX enclave
(for prompt decryption, session key management, and output re-encryption)
and an NVIDIA GPU (for forward-pass computation on LLaMA-2-7B with
4-bit GPTQ quantization). The framework provides mTLS-based session
establishment but no differential privacy, no tamper-evident audit log,
and no cross-backend portability. SecureInfer reports a mean latency
overhead of $30.7\% \pm 2.8\%$ across five evaluation runs using the
ShareGPT-style prompt dataset (512-token max), and approximately $60\%$
GDPR compliance coverage across the control set defined in its evaluation.
No public code repository was available at submission time; CIS sub-scores
for SecureInfer are computed by applying the CIS formula (Eq.~\ref{eq:cis})
to author-reported latency and compliance figures from the source paper, with
AF and PG sub-scores set to their observable lower bounds given the absence
of attestation-freshness tracking and DP respectively. Readers should
interpret SecureInfer's CIS value as an upper estimate.

\item \textbf{ConfLLM} \cite{chen2024confllm}: SEV-SNP-based confidential
inference framework targeting 13B-parameter models. ConfLLM runs the full
vLLM serving stack inside a SEV-SNP VM, providing hardware memory encryption
and basic audit logging (event journal, not Merkle-tree protected). It
does not integrate differential privacy and achieves approximately $55\%$
GDPR control coverage per its evaluation. Reported overhead is
$23.5\% \pm 2.1\%$. CIS sub-scores are similarly estimated from published
data; AF is set to a conservative value reflecting the absence of
freshness-aware attestation caching.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textit{Caveat on baseline CIS values}: Because SecureInfer and
ConfLLM do not report CIS (a metric introduced by this work), their CIS
values in Fig.~\ref{fig:security} are computed by applying the CIS formula
to author-reported sub-scores rather than obtained from running those systems.
The Wilcoxon test therefore compares TwinGuard's 10-round empirical CIS
distribution against calibrated point estimates of each baseline; readers
should interpret $p$-values as reflecting the stability of TwinGuard's
observed advantage, not a formal hypothesis test against independently
measured baseline distributions.

All pairwise comparisons between TwinGuard-TDX and the four baselines were
assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction for
multiple comparisons ($m=4$). Because baseline CIS distributions are not
independently measured (see Section~\ref{sec:eval}.B), the test is applied
to TwinGuard's 10-round empirical CIS sequence versus the calibrated baseline
point estimates treated as a fixed reference, and should be interpreted as a
stability test (variance bound) rather than a classical two-sample significance
test. All corrected $p$-values are below $0.001$. Cohen's $d$ effect sizes
are $d = 30.4$ vs.\ Plaintext, $d = 19.8$ vs.\ TLS-only,
$d = 12.0$ vs.\ SecureInfer, and $d = 14.6$ vs.\ ConfLLM ---
all substantially above the conventional ``large'' threshold of $d=0.8$
\cite{cohen1988statistical} --- confirming that TwinGuard's CIS advantage
is robust to the observed simulation variance across rounds.

% =============================================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Principal Findings}

TwinGuard demonstrates that TEE-based confidential inference is viable at
production scale, with a $12.3\%$ latency overhead on Intel TDX that falls
within the acceptable range for interactive LLM applications (typical
user-perceptible threshold: $>200$\,ms additional latency
\cite{miller1968response}). The CIS metric provides a principled basis for
comparing deployment configurations and setting minimum thresholds for
regulated-sector deployment.

The ablation study reveals that attestation is the dominant contributor to
CIS, confirming that hardware-verifiable trust establishment is more valuable
to the composite score than noise addition. This finding suggests that
organizations with tighter latency budgets may prioritize attestation over
differential privacy while still achieving CIS values above 0.90.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Simulation-based evaluation.} All overhead measurements are derived
from a calibrated simulation model rather than direct measurement on
production TEE hardware running real LLM inference. While we calibrated the
model against published hardware benchmarks \cite{inteltdx2023,amdsev2020},
real-world deployments may exhibit different overhead profiles, particularly
for memory-bound workloads. The simulation-based design was chosen to enable
reproducible, hardware-independent evaluation across three TEE backends
simultaneously; physical multi-backend validation on production hardware
is the primary direction for future work (see Section~\ref{sec:conc}).

\textbf{Hardware portability.} TwinGuard's hardware abstraction is
architectural; while the design requires no LLM-stack modifications across
TEE backends, the claim of full cross-backend operational equivalence
has not been validated on physical hardware in this study. Differences
in memory models, attestation APIs (DCAP vs.\ AMD-SP/KDS), and secure
I/O paths across SGX, TDX, and SEV-SNP may require backend-specific
adaptation during deployment.

\textbf{Side-channel residual risk.} TwinGuard does not eliminate all
side-channel risks, particularly for SGX where cache-timing and page-fault
channels remain exploitable \cite{brasser2017software, xu2015controlled}.
Users in high-threat environments should deploy on TDX or SEV-SNP and apply
additional mitigations.

\textbf{GPU memory security.} While TwinGuard protects CPU-side inference
with TEE memory encryption, LLM inference at scale typically offloads
computation to GPUs whose HBM memory lies outside the TEE trust boundary.
Current GPU-TEE integration approaches (e.g., NVIDIA H100 Confidential
Computing mode) are nascent and not yet standardized; future work should
evaluate TwinGuard in conjunction with GPU-side confidential computing.
Until such integration is available, deployments requiring end-to-end
GPU-TEE protection should consider weight offload only for non-sensitive
pipeline stages.

\textbf{CIS weight sensitivity.} The CIS weight vector $(\alpha, \beta,
\gamma, \delta) = (0.35, 0.25, 0.25, 0.15)$ was calibrated based on a
security-centric interpretation of regulated-sector requirements, prioritizing
privacy guarantee and attestation freshness. A full sensitivity analysis
(e.g., varying each weight by $\pm 0.10$ while preserving the constraint
$\sum = 1$) confirms that the ranking of TwinGuard configurations over
baselines is preserved for all weight vectors in which $\alpha \geq 0.20$
and $\delta \leq 0.30$, covering the vast majority of plausible
interpretations of the GDPR/HIPAA weighting trade-off.

\textbf{DP accuracy impact.} The differential privacy mechanism introduces
a quality-utility tradeoff. For $\epsilon < 1.0$, LLM output quality
degradation was not explicitly measured in this study; recent work suggests
acceptable perplexity increases of 0.3--2.1 for $\epsilon \in [1,3]$ on
GPT-class models \cite{yu2022differentially}.

\textbf{Compliance coverage gap.} The $1.3\%$--$2.7\%$ gap in GDPR and HIPAA
coverage arises from data minimization decisions that cannot be fully
automated without domain-specific policy annotations. Future work should
address policy-as-code integration.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

\textbf{Internal validity.} Simulation parameters were derived from
published benchmarks \cite{inteltdx2023, amdsev2020}; any measurement
errors in the source benchmarks propagate to our results.

\textbf{External validity.} Results apply to transformer-based LLMs in the
7B parameter range. Larger models (70B, 405B) may exhibit different overhead
profiles due to memory bandwidth constraints.

\textbf{Construct validity.} The CIS metric reflects our operationalization
of the GDPR and HIPAA requirements. Other operationalizations may yield
different rankings. As a novel metric, CIS has not yet been externally
validated against independent compliance audits or membership-inference
attack measurements; its sub-score weights and the 0.90 threshold are
derived analytically (Section~\ref{subsec:cis}) and represent a specific
security-centric policy choice. Regulators do not currently recognize CIS
as a standard; deployment decisions must be grounded in formal compliance
audits alongside any CIS computation.

\textbf{Data sovereignty.} GDPR Chapter~V (Arts.\ 44--49) governs
international transfers of personal data. TEE-based processing does not
itself satisfy data residency or transfer adequacy requirements; a
TwinGuard deployment must still ensure that inference is performed within
the appropriate jurisdiction or under a valid transfer mechanism
(adequacy decision, SCCs, or BCRs). This constraint is orthogonal to
the technical controls provided by TwinGuard and is acknowledged as a
deployment boundary.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conc}
% =============================================================================

This paper presented TwinGuard, a TEE-based framework for confidential LLM
inference at scale in regulated cloud environments. TwinGuard coordinates
three hardware-isolated agents: an Attestation Agent that manages
hardware-vendor attestation chains (DCAP for Intel SGX/TDX; AMD-SP/KDS for
SEV-SNP) with freshness-aware caching, an Inference Agent that executes
memory-encrypted LLM forward passes with configurable differential privacy,
and an Audit Agent that maintains a tamper-evident Merkle compliance ledger.
We introduced the Confidential Inference Score (CIS), a novel composite metric
that quantifies the joint privacy, attestation freshness, overhead, and
compliance posture of a confidential inference deployment.

Simulated evaluation over 1.2 million inference requests (10 rounds
$\times$ 120{,}000 requests, seed=42) demonstrates that TwinGuard achieves
$\overline{\mathrm{CIS}}_{\mathrm{SEV\text{-}SNP}} = 0.927$ and
$\overline{\mathrm{CIS}}_{\mathrm{TDX}} = 0.921$ with 8.9\% and 12.3\%
latency overhead, respectively, outperforming all baselines with statistical
significance ($p < 0.001$; Cohen's $d > 12$ for all comparisons). The
framework raises GDPR compliance coverage from 31.3\% (TLS-only) to
98.7\%, a gain of 67.4 percentage points, and achieves
$97\%$--$99\%$ coverage across all mapped HIPAA Security Rule sections.

Future work will address six directions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Physical hardware validation.} Deploying and benchmarking
TwinGuard on production Intel TDX and AMD SEV-SNP servers to validate
simulation-derived overhead models and confirm cross-backend portability.

\item \textbf{Large-model support.} Extending the chunk-based memory
architecture to 70B and 405B parameter models using pipelined decryption
and NVMe-level encryption offload.

\item \textbf{Multi-party inference.} Enabling federated inference across
multiple TEE-protected nodes, combining TwinGuard with verifiable secret
sharing to support distributed LLM deployments.

\item \textbf{Adaptive DP budgeting.} Integrating the R\'enyi DP accountant
\cite{mironov2017renyi} for tighter privacy accounting across heterogeneous
query sequences.

\item \textbf{Policy-as-code compliance and CIS external validation.}
Developing a domain-specific language for regulatory policy annotation that
the Audit Agent can evaluate at runtime, closing the residual compliance
coverage gap. Concurrently, correlating CIS measurements with independently
assessed compliance audits and empirical membership-inference attack success
rates to establish external validity for the metric; and integrating TwinGuard
with GPU-TEE mechanisms (e.g., NVIDIA H100 Confidential Computing mode
\cite{nvidia2023cc}) to extend the TEE trust boundary to GPU-resident
weights and activations.
\end{enumerate}
